**Assumptions made:** The brief ("an antique book restorer") was deliberately light, so I fixed the following to make a compelling, researchable character: contemporary era (lets the living conservation tradition be rendered accurately); London setting (the deepest English-language rare-book trade); literary-mystery genre with the restorer as protagonist; female, age 54, a sole practitioner rather than an institutional conservator (so the moral pressure lands on one person, not a committee). I also distinguish her vocabulary deliberately: she calls herself a **conservator**, and bristles when clients say "restorer," because in the profession the words carry opposite ethics (see §1).

## 1. Real-world grounding (researched)

**The work and its core ethic.** Modern book conservation is governed by two linked principles: **minimal intervention** ("do no harm"; treat only when necessary, weighing every intervention as a calculated risk) and **reversibility** (favour materials and methods that a future conservator can undo without harming the original) [1][2]. This is the practical difference between a *conservator* (stabilise, retain original material, document everything) and an older idea of a *restorer* (make it look new). In rare-book work, decisions are also shaped by an object's competing values — textual, structural, artefactual, and the owner's wishes — so there is rarely one "correct" treatment [2].

**Paper repair — the everyday craft.** Tears and losses are mended with **Japanese tissue (washi)** and **wheat starch paste** because the bond is strong, flexible, and reversible [3][4]. Washi is made from long bast fibres — usually **kozo** (paper mulberry), sometimes **gampi** or **mitsumata** — and ranges from gossamer **tengujo/tengujo-shi** (around 9 g/m², light enough to lay over text without obscuring it) up to heavier sheets like okawara (~60 g/m²) [3]. Wheat starch paste is cooked from a roughly **1:10 ratio (1 g starch to 10 ml water)** and, for tear repair, thinned "to about the consistency of skimmed milk" so the mend stays supple [3]. The tissue patch is cut — or torn, for a feathered, fibrous edge — as small as will do the job [3][4].

**Paper problems and their jargon.** **Foxing** = the brown/yellow/red spotting from mould or metallic impurities and damp [5]. A **tide line** = the dark rim a liquid leaves as it dries, dirt driven deep into the fibres at a stain's edge [5]. **Deacidification** neutralises acidic paper (pH ≤ 6) by depositing an alkaline reserve — often bathing leaves in a **calcium or magnesium bicarbonate** solution [5]. **Humidification** relaxes a sheet with water vapour (plasticising the fibres); **flattening** then follows, the sheet dried between **blotters/felts under weight** [5].

**Leather, vellum, and rot.** Leather bindings (goat/calf/sheepskin) fail at the **hinges/joints** and suffer **red rot** — vegetable-tanned leather degrading to a brittle, powdery, red-staining dust [6][7]. Consolidants: **Klucel-G** (hydroxypropylcellulose in alcohol, leaving an invisible flexible webbing), sometimes top-coated with **SC6000** (acrylic/microcrystalline-wax emulsion) — the "red-rot cocktail" — or **Cellugel** (HPC + isopropanol), which soaks in and leaves no film [6][7]. **Rebacking** (putting a new leather spine on while keeping as much original as possible) is "the most difficult treatment for a book conservator" — lifting, paring, dyeing, covering — slow and expensive; a gentler modern option is rebacking with **toned Japanese or Korean tissue dyed to match** [6]. **Vellum/parchment** distorts at the joints and is treacherous: faked vellum exists, and treating false vellum as real has caused irreparable damage [6].

**Tools of the bench.** A **bone folder** (creasing, smoothing — historically bone or ivory), a **paring knife** (thinning leather), a **lying press** with a **plough** (edge work), a **nipping press** (quick even pressure; platens usually under 11×17 in), a **backing hammer** (rounding and backing the spine) [8][9]. Structural vocabulary: **signatures/sections**, **kettle stitch**, **headbands**, **boards**, **endpapers**, **squares**, **the shoulder/joint**.

**The training path (UK).** Two routes dominate. Formal study at **West Dean College** (Chichester) — a one-year **Graduate Diploma in Conservation Studies specialising in Books & Library Materials**, progressing to an **MA** — taught in a purpose-built bindery with sewing/forwarding kit, board chopper, finishing room and science lab [10][11]; or **Camberwell College of Arts** [12]. There is also a newer **Cultural Heritage Conservator Level 7 apprenticeship** (postgraduate-equivalent) [12]. Professional standing comes from the **Institute of Conservation (Icon)**: accredited members earn the post-nominal **ACR (Accredited Conservator-Restorer)**, assessed against explicit standards plus mandatory CPD and an ethics requirement; there are ~800 ACRs worldwide [13][14].

**The London trade.** The rare-book world clusters in the West End: **Cecil Court** ("Booksellers' Row"), a Victorian pedestrian alley between Charing Cross Road and St Martin's Lane, holds ~20 specialist book/map/print shops [15][16]. The senior trade body is the **Antiquarian Booksellers' Association (ABA)**, founded **1906** — the oldest of its kind — representing 200-plus member businesses [17][18]. This is the milieu a sole conservator like Iris depends on: dealers send her the wrecks they hope to sell, and her signature on a condition report can move a price.

## 2. Physical description (invented, grounded)
Small, compact, deliberate in her movements — the stillness of someone who spends her days not making sudden gestures over fragile things. Reading glasses pushed up into iron-grey hair that's always coming loose from its clip; a watchmaker's loupe on a cord she forgets she's wearing. Hands are her tell: short clean nails, a permanent faint ochre stain on the right thumb and forefinger from leather dye and red-rot dust [6], a thin white scar across the left index finger from a paring knife slip in her West Dean year [8][10]. She dresses in dark, washable, lint-free clothes — wheat-starch paste flecks anything pale [3] — and a long cotton apron with a bone folder in the pocket the way other people carry a phone [8]. Smells, faintly and permanently, of isopropanol, old leather, and library dust.

## 3. Psychology (invented)
- **Core wound:** Eight years ago her husband Daniel, a paper historian, died of an aneurysm mid-sentence at this same bench. She has never moved his stool. Grief taught her, dangerously, that the only damage worth fearing is the irreversible kind.
- **Conscious desire vs. deeper need:** She *wants* to keep working untouched — the bench, the clients, the quiet — believing stillness is integrity. She *needs* to make one irreversible choice on purpose, and survive it; to learn that some things shouldn't be conserved exactly as found.
- **Values:** Reversibility as a near-religious creed [1][2]. Documentation — "if it isn't in the treatment record, it didn't happen." Discretion (the trade runs on it).
- **Contradictions:** She preaches minimal intervention yet privately, brilliantly, *could* commit the perfect forgery — she knows how to tone tissue to vanish into calf [6], age paper, mimic a tide line [5]. The skill that makes her trustworthy is exactly the skill that could make her a faker.
- **Moral line she won't cross:** Putting new material where a buyer would mistake it for original without a record. **The line she will cross:** lying by omission — letting a beautiful object pass as something it isn't, because the alternative is destroying it.

## 4. Backstory (invented, grounded)
Grew up in a damp Norfolk vicarage among foxed parish registers [5]; first "repair" was Sellotape on a hymnal, age nine — a crime she still cites to clients as the original sin of restoration. Read English, drifted into a museum job, then retrained properly: the **Graduate Diploma at West Dean**, the scar, the MA [10][11]. Met Daniel over a disbound 17th-century herbal. They opened the Cecil Court bench together; she did the hands, he did the histories [15]. She earned her **ACR** the year before he died [13]; the assessment portfolio is the last document with both their handwriting in it. Since then she has worked alone, refusing apprentices, refusing the institutional jobs that would dilute the bench into a committee.

## 5. Voice & mannerisms (invented, grounded)
Precise, dry, allergic to overstatement. Slips into trade shorthand and then translates it, half-apologetically, for clients. Corrects "restore" to "conserve" so reflexively it's a verbal tic. Narrates her own hands ("there — feel the shoulder give") as if the book were a third person in the room. Goes very quiet, not angry, when she disapproves.

- *"It's not a restorer you want, it's a conservator. A restorer makes it lie to you. I just keep it from getting worse — and I write down every single thing I do, so the next pair of hands can undo me."*
- *"Wheat starch, kozo tissue, paste like skimmed milk. Strong, supple, and reversible. Everything I put into a book, someone in fifty years can take back out. That's the whole job. That's the only promise I make."*
- *"Red rot doesn't tear, dear. It surrenders. You touch the spine and it comes away on your fingers like rust off an old gate — and then it's on everything you own."*

## 6. Relationships (invented, grounded)
- **Daniel Vane (dec.)** — late husband, paper historian. The absence that organises her days; his stool, his marginalia, his unfinished provenance note on the disputed book.
- **Hugh Marchmont** — Cecil Court dealer, ABA member, decades of mutual reliance [15][17]. He brings her the wrecks and, this time, the manuscript whose authenticity her report will make or break. Affection shading into a conflict of interest neither names.
- **Priya Anand** — a hungry, gifted West Dean graduate Iris keeps refusing to take on [10]. The apprentice she won't train is the conscience (and successor) she's avoiding.
- **The book itself** — a damaged volume of contested provenance; functionally a character, the thing she must decide whether to make whole, expose, or let pass.

## 7. Character arc (invented)
**Start:** Frozen in immaculate practice — reversibility as a way to never truly decide anything, grief sealed behind professional ethics. **Pressure:** A volume comes in whose value depends on a provenance she comes to doubt; saving the *object* and telling the truth about the *book* turn out to be incompatible, and Marchmont's livelihood — and her own — ride on her report [2][17]. She realises she has the skill to make the lie permanent and perfect. **End:** She makes one deliberate, irreversible choice — and signs her name to it. She finally takes Priya on, moves Daniel's stool, and accepts that some interventions can't, and shouldn't, be undone.

## 8. Story function & theme (invented)
Iris embodies the book's central question: **what do we owe the truth of an object versus its survival?** Her professional creed — minimal intervention, total reversibility, document everything — is tested against grief and complicity, where life refuses to be reversible and silence is its own intervention. The conservator who never alters anything must learn that choosing *not* to act is also a choice with consequences.

## 9. Authenticity details to use
- She says **"conserve," never "restore,"** and visibly winces at the latter — the words carry opposite ethics [1][2].
- **Wheat starch paste thinned "to the consistency of skimmed milk"** and **kozo/tengujo tissue** torn (not cut) for a feathered edge [3][4].
- **Red rot** as sensory texture: a powdery red dust that "comes away like rust" and stains everything; treated with **Klucel-G**, **SC6000**, or **Cellugel** [6][7].
- A **bone folder in the apron pocket "the way other people carry a phone"** [8]; a paring-knife scar from her West Dean training year [8][10].
- The **post-nominal ACR** after her name and the quiet authority of an **Icon-accredited** condition report that can move a price [13][14].
- **Foxing**, **tide lines**, **deacidification** (magnesium bicarbonate bath), **rebacking** as "the hardest treatment there is," **kettle stitch**, **headbands**, **the shoulder/joint** — drop sparingly for texture [5][6][8].
- Setting: a first-floor bench off **Cecil Court**, dealers from the **ABA** (est. 1906) sending up the wrecks [15][17].

## 10. Open questions for the author
1. **The book's nature.** Is the contested volume an outright modern forgery, a genuine work with a faked provenance/association, or authentic but "improved" by a previous restorer? Each implicates Iris differently.
2. **How far does she go?** Does she actually perform the perfect, undetectable intervention (becoming complicit), refuse and expose it (destroying Marchmont), or find a third path — and is that third path honest or a rationalisation?
3. **POV and timeframe.** Tight first-person present at the bench, or a dual timeline braiding Daniel's death with the present case? The reversibility theme lands hardest if grief and forgery share the page.
4. **Iris's own past.** Has she ever crossed the line before — a youthful "improvement" she's buried — so this isn't her first irreversible act, only the first she admits?
5. **Priya's role.** Witness, foil, or the one who ultimately catches Iris out? Whether Iris's secret is exposed by the apprentice changes the ending's moral temperature.
